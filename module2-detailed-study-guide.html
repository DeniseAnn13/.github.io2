Vision

1. Draw a diagram of the visual pathways, label the parts, and indicate the visual fields.

        (right visual field)             (left visual field)

Visual-field rule: Each hemisphere processes the opposite visual field. Ganglion axons leave the retina through the optic nerve, nasal retinal fibers cross at the optic chiasm, temporal fibers remain ipsilateral, and the signal proceeds through the LGN to cortex.

2. List the types of retinal cells and describe their function.

  • Photoreceptors: rods support dim-light and peripheral detection; cones support color and fine detail.
  • Bipolar cells: relay photoreceptor signals and contribute to light/dark center-surround organization.
  • Ganglion cells: integrate bipolar/amacrine input and send action potentials through the optic nerve.
  • Horizontal cells: connect photoreceptors and influence lateral inhibition and contrast.
  • Amacrine cells: connect nearby bipolar and ganglion cells and help encode motion, timing, and contrast.
  • Pigment epithelium: absorbs scattered light and regenerates/supports photopigments.

3. Describe the trichromatic theory and relate it to the anatomy of the eye.

Retinal cones contain photopigments most sensitive to short (S), medium (M), or long (L) wavelengths. Color perception begins with the relative activity of these three cone populations. The brain combines that activity to create hue and brightness. This first stage is trichromatic, although later opponent processing—red/green and blue/yellow—refines color perception.

4. How does visual-cortex organization support perception of complex objects?

V1 is retinotopically organized and contains neurons tuned to location, orientation, contrast, and direction. Information becomes increasingly complex in higher visual areas. The ventral stream toward temporal cortex supports object/face identification (the “what” pathway), whereas the dorsal stream toward parietal cortex supports motion and spatial relationships (the “where/how” pathway). The brain combines these representations rather than seeing only isolated edges.

5. Why is experience necessary for visual-system development?

During sensitive periods, experience refines retinal-to-cortex connections, ocular dominance, orientation tuning, depth perception, and binocular integration. Abnormal or absent input can cause lasting deficits, whereas recovery is easier while connections remain plastic. Genetics provides initial circuitry, but normal patterned light and coordinated eye input shape its final organization.

Hearing

1. Draw the middle ear and cochlea and describe transduction.

                            basilar membrane vibrates
                                          ↓

                  stereocilia bend → ion channels open
                                          ↓
                     receptor potential/transmitter release
                                          ↓

The tympanic membrane collects air pressure waves. The ossicles amplify and transfer vibration to the oval window. Fluid motion displaces the basilar membrane and bends stereocilia. Mechanical bending opens ion channels and produces receptor activity; hair cells transmit to auditory-nerve fibers.

2. Draw the auditory pathway and explain pitch or sound localization.

Hair cells → auditory nerve → cochlear nuclei
→ superior olivary complex → inferior colliculus
→ medial geniculate nucleus → primary auditory cortex

Pitch: is represented both by the place of maximal basilar-membrane activity (place coding) and, especially for lower frequencies, by the timing of neural firing (temporal coding).

Localization: The superior olivary complex compares interaural time differences and level differences. The pinna provides monaural spectral cues that help determine front/back and elevation.

Touch/Pain

1. List and describe tactile receptors.

  • Merkel discs: sustained pressure and fine spatial detail.
  • Meissner corpuscles: light touch and low-frequency vibration.
  • Pacinian corpuscles: deep pressure and rapid high-frequency vibration.
  • Ruffini endings: skin stretch and sustained pressure.
  • Hair-follicle receptors: movement of hairs.
  • Free nerve endings: touch, temperature, itch, or tissue danger.

2. Name primary nociceptive fibers and roles.

  • A-delta fibers: thinly myelinated, faster, sharp and localized first pain.
  • C fibers: unmyelinated, slower, dull, aching, burning second pain.

3. Name sensorimotor cortical areas and functions.

  • S1: somatosensory maps and tactile discrimination.
  • S2: integration of touch across sides and objects.
  • Posterior parietal cortex: sensory-guided spatial attention and action.
  • M1: sends cortical commands for voluntary movement.
  • Premotor cortex: prepares externally cued movement.
  • SMA: sequences and plans internally generated movement.

4. Distinguish dorsal and ventral pathways.

Somatosensory dorsal-column route: fine touch, vibration, and proprioception ascend in dorsal columns, cross in the medulla, relay through thalamus, and reach cortex.

Anterolateral/ventral route: pain, temperature, and crude touch cross near spinal entry, ascend in the anterolateral spinal cord, and relay to thalamus/cortex.

Visual-stream distinction: The dorsal visual stream is “where/how,” while the ventral is “what.”

Chemical Senses

1. List and describe taste transducers.

  • Sweet: T1R2/T1R3 G-protein-coupled receptor complex.
  • Umami: T1R1/T1R3 complex detects amino acids such as glutamate.
  • Bitter: T2R receptor family detects diverse bitter compounds.
  • Sour: acidity is detected by a proton-sensitive mechanism including OTOP1.
  • Salty: ion entry and sodium-sensitive mechanisms detect salt.

2. How do 4–5 receptor qualities create many tastes?

Foods activate mixtures of taste receptors at different intensities. Neural population patterns encode combinations, while smell, temperature, texture, irritation, learning, and memory combine with taste to create many flavor experiences.

3. List and describe olfactory transducers.

Odor molecules bind to diverse G-protein-coupled receptor types on olfactory sensory neurons. Golf and second messengers produce depolarization. Combinations of activated receptors create population codes that the olfactory bulb organizes and sends through the olfactory pathway.

Motor Activity

1. Example motor reflex and voluntary modification.

The knee-jerk/stretch reflex begins when tendon tap stretches a muscle, activates muscle-spindle afferents, and monosynaptically excites a motor neuron in the spinal cord. The reflex is automatic but descending cortical and brainstem input can alter gain according to posture, arousal, or intentional context.

2. How does the brain control and regulate movement?

Posterior parietal cortex translates goals and sensory context into plans; premotor/SMA areas prepare movement; M1 sends commands; brainstem and spinal circuits execute and maintain posture; sensory feedback corrects errors. Basal ganglia select actions and cerebellum fine-tune timing and accuracy.

3. Roles of sensorimotor cortical areas.

M1: execution; premotor: external-cue preparation; SMA: sequences/internal initiation; posterior parietal: sensory integration and goal-directed planning; S1: tactile feedback and body representation.

4. Role of the cerebellum.

The cerebellum compares planned and executed movement, refines timing and force, supports balance and posture, and supports motor learning. Damage produces ataxia, tremor during action, and poor coordination rather than necessarily complete paralysis.

5. Roles of major motor pathways.

  • Corticospinal: voluntary limb and skilled finger movement.
  • Corticobulbar: cranial motor nuclei for face, jaw, tongue, and head.
  • Brainstem pathways: posture, balance, tone, and orienting.

6. Role of the basal ganglia.

Basal ganglia select desired actions, suppress competitors, initiate movement, and shape learning/reward. Cortex and thalamus participate in loops through the striatum and pallidal output structures.

8. Basal-ganglia damage in the two diseases.

Parkinson’s reduces dopamine to the striatum. With reduced direct-pathway excitation and increased indirect-pathway influence, the basal-ganglia output makes initiating movement difficult. Huntington’s causes progressive striatal degeneration, especially involving indirect-pathway neurons early, so competing movements are less inhibited and chorea appears.

Regulation

1. Compare homeostasis and allostasis.

Homeostasis defends fixed set points: if temperature rises, sweating lowers it. Allostasis anticipates changing demands and varies set points: heart rate and hormones rise before a stressor or exercise. Allostasis is flexible control; homeostasis is stability around a target.

2. Give examples.

Homeostatic: shivering or sweating restores body temperature. Allostatic: the heart rate and stress-hormone levels increase before an exam or race.

3. Behavioral temperature regulation.

Examples include seeking shade, wearing or removing clothing, huddling, turning on heat or AC, changing posture, or choosing a warm/cool environment.

4. What behaviors might cold-triggered hypothalamic responses alter?

Seeking warmth, huddling, reducing exposure, adding clothing, curling up, increasing activity, and food seeking can accompany autonomic responses such as shivering and constricted skin vessels.

5. Name and describe two thirst types.

Osmotic thirst: follows concentrated extracellular fluid and is detected by hypothalamic osmosensory areas.

Hypovolemic thirst: follows reduced blood volume/pressure and is detected by cardiovascular and kidney signals.

6. Receptors and chemical messengers.

  • Osmotic: osmoreceptors; vasopressin/ADH conserves water.
  • Volume/pressure: baroreceptors and kidney sensors; renin → angiotensin II promotes thirst and vasopressin/aldosterone responses.
  • Aldosterone: promotes sodium conservation, which helps retain fluid.

7. When to drink water or sports drinks?

Drink water for routine hydration and shorter exercise. Sports drinks can help during prolonged, intense exercise or heavy sweat loss by replacing fluid, sodium, and carbohydrate. They are usually unnecessary for ordinary daily activity and contain sugar.

8. Hypothalamic areas that regulate food intake.

Arcuate nucleus: integrates leptin, insulin, ghrelin, nutrients, and gut signals. NPY/AgRP neurons generally promote hunger; POMC/CART neurons generally promote satiety.

Lateral hypothalamic region: broadly promotes feeding and arousal, while ventromedial hypothalamus and nearby regions contribute to satiety and energy balance. These areas communicate with brainstem and cortical reward circuits.

9. Psychological and genetic factors contributing to obesity.

Genetic variation affects appetite, metabolism, fat storage, reward, and satiety. Psychological factors include stress, mood, sleep, emotional eating, habits, and learned food reward. Environment and socioeconomic conditions influence access, portion sizes, and activity.

10. How allostasis makes weight loss difficult.

After weight loss, the body may anticipate danger of starvation and reduce expenditure, raise hunger, and reduce fullness. Leptin may fall, ghrelin can rise, and learned reward cues may persist. These allostatic adaptations defend the established weight and can cause regain.

Sleep

1. Physiological methods of shifting the sleep cycle.

Timed bright light is the strongest external intervention. Morning light shifts the clock earlier; evening light may delay it. Consistent sleep/wake times, melatonin, exercise timing, and meal timing reinforce or shift circadian rhythms.

2. Disadvantages of sleeping pills.

Risks include next-day drowsiness, impaired coordination, memory problems, tolerance, dependence, rebound insomnia, withdrawal, altered sleep architecture, and drug interactions. Effectiveness and risk depend on the medication.

3. How sleep drugs affect receptors and arousal.

  • Benzodiazepines/Z-drugs: enhance GABA-A inhibition and sedate.
  • Melatonin drugs: activate melatonin receptors to signal biological night.
  • Orexin antagonists: block orexin-driven wakefulness.
  • Antihistamines: block histamine H1 arousal signaling.
  • Caffeine: blocks adenosine receptors and reduces sleep pressure.

4. Why do we sleep? Two theories.

Restoration theory: sleep repairs tissue, supports metabolism, immune function, synaptic regulation, and memory.

Adaptive/inactivity theory: sleep conserves energy and prevents activity during dangerous or inefficient periods.

5. Why do we dream? Two theories.

Activation-synthesis: cortex creates narrative meaning from internally generated brainstem/REM activity.

Cognitive/continuity: dreaming reflects memory, emotion, expectations, and unresolved waking concerns; it can be unusual because logical constraints and sensory input change.
