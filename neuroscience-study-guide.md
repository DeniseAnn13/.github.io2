# Neuroscience Study Guide

## 1. Introduction: What is neuroscience?

**Neuroscience** is the scientific study of the nervous system: its structure, function, development, chemistry, disorders, and relationship to behavior and experience. It combines methods from biology, psychology, medicine, chemistry, physics, and computation. Neuroscientists study questions at several levels, from molecules and cells to circuits, behavior, and cognition.

### How do we study the living brain?

| Method | How it works | What it measures well | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CAT/CT (computed axial tomography)** | Rotating X-rays are collected from many angles and a computer reconstructs cross-sectional images. | Anatomy and tissue-density differences; useful for detecting bleeding, fractures, and large structural changes. | Uses ionizing radiation; less detailed soft-tissue contrast than MRI; does not directly measure neural activity. |
| **PET (positron emission tomography)** | A small amount of radioactive tracer is administered. Detectors map tracer distribution as it emits positrons; the tracer may track glucose use, blood flow, or a particular molecule/receptor. | A biochemical or metabolic process, depending on the tracer. | Radiation exposure; relatively low spatial and temporal resolution; tracer uptake is an indirect measure and scans are comparatively slow. |
| **MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)** | Strong magnetic fields and radiofrequency pulses produce signals from hydrogen nuclei; differences in those signals are reconstructed into images. | Detailed structural images and tissue contrast, without ionizing radiation. | Structural MRI does not directly measure neuronal firing; scans can be sensitive to movement and some implants are contraindicated. |

**Important distinction:** Functional MRI (**fMRI**) is an MRI technique that estimates changes in blood oxygenation and flow (the BOLD signal) associated with neural activity. It is an indirect, delayed measure—not a direct recording of action potentials. CAT/CT, PET, and MRI are not interchangeable: they use different physical principles and answer different questions.

### Lesions, drugs, and genetic manipulations

| Approach | How it works | What it can tell us | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lesion studies** | Researchers study behavior or brain function after a naturally occurring injury or a carefully controlled experimental disruption/removal of tissue. | If a function is reliably impaired, the affected area or pathway may be necessary for that function. Comparisons before/after or across lesion locations can reveal functional organization. | Damage may affect fibers passing through an area as well as local cells; brain areas work in networks; compensation and differences in lesion size complicate conclusions. A lesion alone rarely proves a region is sufficient for a behavior. |
| **Drug studies** | A drug changes the activity of receptors, transporters, enzymes, or other targets; effects are measured in cells, circuits, or behavior. | How changing a chemical signaling system influences a process; drugs can help test mechanisms and identify potential treatments. | Drugs can act at multiple targets and in multiple body systems. Dose, timing, delivery, and side effects matter; an effect does not by itself identify the drug's exact site of action. |
| **Genetic manipulations** | A gene or its expression is altered, added, or silenced, sometimes in selected cell types or at selected times. | Whether a gene or gene product contributes to development, cell function, or behavior; targeted approaches can test causal hypotheses. | Developmental compensation, off-target effects, and differences between species can complicate interpretation. Genes usually influence risk and biological processes rather than determining complex behavior on their own. |

A strong explanation of brain function usually combines methods and converging evidence rather than relying on one result.

### Common brain myths—and the evidence-based view

- **“We use only 10% of our brain.”** False. Brain activity and imaging show that many regions have functions, and even at rest the brain is active. Not every neuron is maximally active at once, but that is different from unused brain tissue.
- **“People are either left-brained or right-brained.”** The hemispheres have some specializations, but most complex tasks rely on networks in both hemispheres. People are not divided into simple, fixed brain types.
- **“A person is a visual, auditory, or kinesthetic learner, so teaching should match that type.”** People may have preferences, but evidence does not support the claim that matching instruction to a fixed learning-style label reliably improves learning. Use methods suited to the content and practice retrieval and feedback.
- **“Adult brains cannot change.”** The brain retains capacity for plasticity throughout life, though the type and extent of plasticity vary with age, experience, and the system involved.
- **“Brain scans can read thoughts or prove what someone is thinking.”** Most scans measure indirect signals with limits in resolution and interpretation. A scan does not provide a simple, definitive translation of an individual's private thoughts.
- **“A single brain area controls a complex behavior.”** Some functions depend strongly on particular structures, but complex behavior generally emerges from interacting networks and is shaped by the body and environment.

## 2. Neuroanatomy

### Divisions of the nervous system

```text
Nervous system
├── Central nervous system (CNS)
│   ├── Brain       — integrates information; perception, thought, emotion, action
│   └── Spinal cord — relays signals and coordinates many reflexes
└── Peripheral nervous system (PNS)
    ├── Sensory (afferent) division — carries information from receptors toward CNS
    └── Motor (efferent) division  — carries commands from CNS to effectors
        ├── Somatic nervous system      — skeletal muscle; voluntary movement and reflexes
        └── Autonomic nervous system    — glands, smooth muscle, cardiac muscle; mostly involuntary
            ├── Sympathetic division
            ├── Parasympathetic division
            └── Enteric networks (intrinsic circuits of the gut)
```

**Afferent arrives** at the CNS; **efferent exits** the CNS.

### Sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions

These divisions often influence the same organs in different, coordinated ways; neither is simply “on” or “off.”

| Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
|---|---|
| Supports mobilization and energy use in demanding situations (“fight, flight, or freeze”). | Supports maintenance, recovery, and digestion (“rest and digest”). |
| Typically increases heart rate and redirects resources toward immediate action. | Typically slows heart rate and supports digestion and routine maintenance. |
| Often dilates pupils and bronchi and reduces digestive activity during acute demands. | Often constricts pupils and supports digestive activity. |
| Uses a two-neuron pathway: preganglionic neuron from CNS to a ganglion, then postganglionic neuron to target. | Also uses a two-neuron pathway; ganglia are generally near or within target organs. |

Both divisions use acetylcholine at autonomic ganglia. Most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine; most parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine. Important exceptions exist.

Dorsal and ventral roots join to form a mixed spinal nerve.

### Major divisions of the brain

```text
Brain
├── Cerebrum (telencephalon) — perception, voluntary action, language, memory, planning
│   ├── Cerebral cortex — layered outer tissue; many higher-order functions
│   ├── Basal ganglia — action selection, movement, habits and learning
│   └── Limbic-related structures — memory, emotion, motivation (distributed network)
├── Diencephalon
│   ├── Thalamus — major relay and coordination hub for many signals to cortex
│   └── Hypothalamus — homeostasis, endocrine and autonomic regulation, motivated behavior
├── Brainstem
│   ├── Midbrain — movement, orienting, and sensory processing functions
│   ├── Pons — relay and contributes to sleep, arousal, and movement functions
│   └── Medulla — vital autonomic functions, including breathing and cardiovascular control
└── Cerebellum — coordination, timing, balance, motor learning; also contributes to cognition
```

These are broad descriptions: functions are distributed and structures have multiple roles.

### Side view: cerebral lobes

- **Frontal lobe:** planning, decision-making, cognitive control, voluntary movement; parts support speech production.
- **Parietal lobe:** integrates bodily sensations and spatial information; supports attention and sensorimotor integration.

Lobe boundaries and functions are simplified; complex abilities depend on connected regions across lobes.

## 3. Cells of the nervous system: neurons and glia

### General animal cell

```text
        ______________________________________
       /                                      \
      |   ~~~ rough ER + ribosomes             |
      |       _________       () mitochondrion |
      |      / nucleus \                      |
      |      \_________/   Golgi apparatus     |
      |   smooth ER         ≋≋≋                 |
      |   cytoplasm                             |
       \______________________________________/
             cell membrane (boundary)
```

- **Cell membrane:** selective boundary that maintains gradients and contains proteins such as channels, pumps, and receptors.
- **Nucleus:** DNA storage and gene regulation.
- **Ribosomes/rough ER:** protein synthesis and processing.
- **Smooth ER:** lipid synthesis and other cell functions; in neurons, ER also stores calcium.
- **Golgi apparatus:** modifies, sorts, and packages proteins and lipids.
- **Mitochondria:** produce much of the cell's usable energy and participate in other signaling processes.
- **Cytoplasm:** interior fluid and structures in which many cellular reactions occur.

### Neuron and direction of signaling

```text
 incoming signals                                              output
      ↓                                                          ↓
 dendrites → [cell body / soma] → axon hillock → axon =======→ axon terminals
                 nucleus                         myelin      (synaptic boutons)
                                                  ↑  ↑
                                         nodes of Ranvier

Typical action-potential propagation: axon hillock → along axon → terminals
```

**Dendrites** receive many inputs; the **soma** integrates cellular activity; the **axon initial segment/hillock** is a common site of action-potential initiation; the **axon** conducts action potentials; **terminals** communicate with target cells. Neurons vary in shape and signaling direction, so this is a common pattern rather than a universal rule.

### Glia and myelin

| Glial cell | Main roles |
|---|---|
| **Astrocytes (CNS)** | Support neurons, regulate extracellular ions and neurotransmitters, provide metabolic support, influence synapses, and help maintain the blood–brain barrier. |
| **Oligodendrocytes (CNS)** | Make myelin; one oligodendrocyte can myelinate segments on multiple axons. |
| **Schwann cells (PNS)** | Make myelin around PNS axons; one myelinating Schwann cell wraps one axon segment. Non-myelinating Schwann cells also support small axons. |
| **Microglia (CNS)** | Immune surveillance, debris clearance, and responses to injury or infection; also influence circuit remodeling. |
| **Ependymal cells (CNS)** | Line the brain ventricles and central canal; specialized ependymal cells contribute to cerebrospinal-fluid production and circulation. |
| **Satellite glia (PNS)** | Surround neuron cell bodies in peripheral ganglia and regulate their local environment. |

**Myelin** is produced by oligodendrocytes in the CNS and Schwann cells in the PNS.

### Blood–brain barrier (BBB)

The BBB is a selective interface between blood and brain tissue. Its core physical barrier is formed by **endothelial cells** lining brain capillaries, joined by **tight junctions** that limit passage between cells. **Pericytes**, the **basement membrane**, and **astrocyte end-feet** support and regulate the barrier; transport proteins control the movement of nutrients and other substances across it.

**Function:** helps stabilize the brain's chemical environment, admits needed substances through selective transport, and restricts many toxins, pathogens, and blood-borne fluctuations. It is selective, not an absolute wall: some substances cross, some have specific transporters, and barrier properties vary by region and condition.

## 4. Within cells: electrical properties of neurons

### Resting membrane potential

A typical neuron's resting membrane potential is often around **−70 mV**, though it varies by cell. The inside is negative relative to the outside. The voltage comes from **unequal ion distributions** and **selective membrane permeability**, not from one ion pump directly “pushing” a voltage into the cell.

1. The **Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase** uses ATP to move **3 Na⁺ out** and **2 K⁺ in**, maintaining sodium and potassium gradients over time (and making a small direct electrogenic contribution).
2. At rest, the membrane is generally more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺ because of K⁺ leak channels. K⁺ tends to diffuse out down its concentration gradient, leaving behind relatively negative charge.
3. Electrical forces oppose further K⁺ efflux as the inside becomes negative. The balance of chemical and electrical forces, plus permeability to other ions (including Cl⁻ and a small Na⁺ leak), sets the resting voltage.

### Action potential graph

```text
Membrane

            depolarization       repolarization
                     |<-- absolute refractory -->|
                                        |<-- relative refractory -->|
```

Threshold is commonly near −55 mV in simplified diagrams, but it varies. **Depolarization** means the membrane potential becomes less negative; **repolarization** returns it toward rest; **hyperpolarization/undershoot** is a brief voltage more negative than resting potential. The **absolute refractory period** is when another action potential cannot be initiated; the **relative refractory period** is when a stronger-than-usual input may be needed.

### How an action potential is generated

1. Inputs produce graded voltage changes in the dendrites and soma. If depolarization at the axon initial segment reaches threshold, an action potential begins.
2. Voltage-gated **Na⁺ channels open** rapidly. Na⁺ enters down its electrochemical gradient, producing the rising phase.
3. Na⁺ channels **inactivate**; voltage-gated **K⁺ channels open** more slowly. K⁺ leaves, producing repolarization.
4. K⁺ channels close gradually, so continued K⁺ efflux can cause the after-hyperpolarization.
5. Channel states reset. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump and leak pathways maintain/re-establish ion gradients over time; the pump is not what repolarizes each individual action potential.
6. Local current depolarizes the next axon segment, propagating the action potential. Refractoriness behind the wave helps prevent backward re-excitation.

Action potentials are **all-or-none** once threshold is reached; stronger sustained input is commonly represented by changes in firing rate and timing, not by larger individual action potentials.

### Myelin and saltatory conduction

Myelin electrically insulates axon segments, reducing current leakage and allowing depolarizing current to spread farther and faster beneath the sheath. Voltage-gated channels are concentrated at **nodes of Ranvier**. In myelinated axons, the action potential is regenerated at successive nodes—often described as **saltatory conduction** (“jumping” node to node). In unmyelinated axons, the action potential is regenerated continuously along adjacent membrane. Saltatory conduction is generally faster and more energy-efficient, though speed also depends on axon diameter and other properties.

## 5. Between cells: chemical signaling

### Reflex arc

```text
Stimulus → sensory receptor → sensory (afferent) neuron
                                  ↓ enters spinal cord through dorsal root
                         interneuron(s) in CNS (may be absent in a simple reflex)
                                  ↓
                         motor (efferent) neuron exits via ventral root
                                  ↓
                         effector (muscle or gland) → response
```

A reflex is a rapid, organized response. The brain can receive information during a spinal reflex, but the initial response can be coordinated by spinal circuits. Some reflexes are monosynaptic; many include one or more interneurons.

### Chemical synapse and signaling sequence

```text
Presynaptic neuron                                  Postsynaptic cell
axon terminal                                      dendrite / soma
┌──────────────────────┐                         ┌────��──────────────┐
│ synaptic vesicles ○○  │                         │ receptors  Y Y    │
│ voltage-gated Ca²⁺ → │  neurotransmitter       │ ion channels /    │
│ channels             │  • • • across cleft →   │ signaling proteins│
└──────────────────────┘     synaptic cleft      └───────────────────┘
          presynaptic membrane                  postsynaptic membrane
```

1. An action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal.
2. Depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; Ca²⁺ enters the terminal.
3. Ca²⁺ triggers vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release neurotransmitter by exocytosis.
4. Neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds receptors on the postsynaptic cell (or sometimes presynaptic receptors).
5. Receptor activation changes ion flow or intracellular signaling, producing an effect that may be excitatory, inhibitory, or modulatory depending on receptor and cell.
6. Signaling ends as transmitter is cleared by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, diffusion, or a combination. Vesicle components are recycled.

### Spatial and temporal summation

- **Spatial summation:** postsynaptic potentials from different synapses arrive around the same time and combine at the neuron, especially near the axon initial segment.
- **Temporal summation:** postsynaptic potentials from the same synapse arrive close together in time and overlap.

Excitatory and inhibitory inputs both contribute. Whether threshold is reached depends on their timing, location, size, and the neuron's membrane properties.

### Ionotropic receptor

```text
outside / synaptic cleft
     neurotransmitter ● binds
                    ↓
             ┌─────────────┐
membrane  ────┤ ligand-gated├────
             │ ion channel │   → ions pass when open
             └──────┬──────┘
                    ↓
inside cell: ion flow changes membrane potential
```

An **ionotropic receptor** is a ligand-gated ion channel. Neurotransmitter binding directly changes channel opening, allowing selected ions to cross the membrane. The response is usually relatively fast. Its effect depends on which ions pass and the cell's ion gradients: it can depolarize, hyperpolarize, or otherwise alter excitability.

### Metabotropic receptor

```text
outside / synaptic cleft
       neurotransmitter ●
                    ↓
membrane  ────────[ receptor ]────────
                       │
                       ▼
                 G protein (Gα, Gβγ)
                       │
             ┌─────────┴─────────┐
             ▼                   ▼
       ion channel change   second messenger / enzyme
                            (e.g., cAMP pathway)
             └─────────┬─────────┘
                       ▼
           altered cell activity or gene/protein function
```

A **metabotropic receptor** does not itself form an ion channel. Binding activates intracellular signaling, often through a G protein, which can directly regulate ion channels or trigger second-messenger cascades. Effects are often slower in onset and longer-lasting than ionotropic effects, and can amplify or modulate cellular responses.
