12 Short-Answer Practice Questions

Practice answering each question in a few clear sentences, or draw and label a diagram where requested. These questions cover the study-guide topics; they are practice, not a prediction of the exact exam.

How to practice: Answer all 12 without looking at the key points. Then expand “Check key points” to review. For diagram questions, label structures and show information flow with arrows.

1. Compare CAT/CT, PET, and MRI.

Briefly explain how each works, what it measures best, and one limitation.

Check key points
  • CAT/CT reconstructs anatomy from X-rays; useful for structural density differences, but uses ionizing radiation.
  • PET maps a radioactive tracer to measure a selected metabolic or biochemical process; involves radiation and has relatively low spatial and temporal resolution.
  • MRI uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency signals for detailed structural images without ionizing radiation; structural MRI does not directly record neural firing.
  • fMRI estimates activity indirectly through blood-oxygenation (BOLD) changes.

2. Compare lesion, drug, and genetic studies.

What does each method change, and what can its results tell us?

Check key points
  • Lesions disrupt tissue; impaired function suggests the affected area or pathway may be necessary, but network damage and compensation are caveats.
  • Drugs alter chemical signaling; effects reveal how targeted systems contribute, but drugs can have multiple targets and side effects.
  • Genetic manipulations alter gene expression; they test gene contributions, with developmental compensation and off-target effects as cautions.
  • Converging evidence from multiple methods strengthens conclusions.

3. Correct two common myths about the brain.

Name two myths and give an evidence-based correction for each.

Check key points

Examples: people use far more than 10% of the brain; both hemispheres contribute to most complex tasks despite some specialization; plasticity continues in adulthood; brain scans measure indirect signals and do not simply read thoughts; complex functions rely on networks.

4. Diagram or describe the divisions of the nervous system.

Include CNS, PNS, sensory/afferent, motor/efferent, somatic, and autonomic branches.

Check key points

CNS = brain and spinal cord. PNS carries information between the CNS and body. Sensory/afferent signals travel toward the CNS; motor/efferent commands travel away. The motor division includes somatic (skeletal muscle) and autonomic (organs, glands, smooth and cardiac muscle); autonomic includes sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric divisions.

5. Contrast sympathetic and parasympathetic activity.

Give their general roles and one example of how each affects the body.

Check key points

Sympathetic activity mobilizes resources for demands (“fight or flight”); it commonly increases heart rate and reduces digestive activity. Parasympathetic activity supports maintenance and digestion (“rest and digest”); it commonly slows heart rate and supports digestion. The divisions coordinate rather than acting as a simple on/off switch.

6. Draw a transverse spinal-cord section and label key structures.

Include dorsal and ventral sides, gray and white matter, horns, central canal, and roots.

Check key points

Show butterfly-shaped gray matter inside surrounding white matter. Label dorsal horns (sensory processing), ventral horns (motor neuron cell bodies), central canal, dorsal sensory root and dorsal root ganglion, and ventral motor root. Roots join to form a spinal nerve. Lateral horns contain autonomic neurons at relevant spinal levels.

7. Name major brain divisions and state a broad function for each.

Include cerebrum, diencephalon, brainstem, and cerebellum.

Check key points
  • Cerebrum: perception, voluntary action, memory, language, and planning.
  • Diencephalon: thalamic relay and hypothalamic homeostasis/endocrine/autonomic regulation.
  • Brainstem: relay, arousal, and vital autonomic functions.
  • Cerebellum: coordination, balance, timing, and motor learning.

8. Label the four cerebral lobes and give a general function for each.

A simple side-view drawing is encouraged.

Check key points
  • Frontal: planning, executive control, and voluntary movement.
  • Parietal: bodily sensation and spatial processing.
  • Temporal: hearing, language, and memory-related functions.
  • Occipital: vision.

Complex functions involve connected networks, not only one lobe.

9. Identify neuron parts, action-potential direction, and myelinating glia.

Include dendrites, soma, axon initial segment, axon, terminals, CNS/PNS myelin cells, and one other glial function.

Check key points

Typical signal flow: dendrites → soma → axon initial segment → axon → terminals. Oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS axons; Schwann cells myelinate PNS axons. Astrocytes support neurons and help regulate the BBB; microglia provide immune surveillance.

10. Explain the resting potential and stages of an action potential.

Mention ion gradients, the Na⁺/K⁺ pump, threshold, channels, and refractory periods.

Check key points
  • Unequal ion distributions and selective permeability—especially K⁺ leak—produce a negative resting voltage. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains gradients (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in).
  • At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; Na⁺ influx depolarizes.
  • Na⁺ channels inactivate and K⁺ channels open; K⁺ efflux repolarizes and may cause undershoot/hyperpolarization.
  • Absolute refractory: another spike cannot start; relative refractory: a stronger input may be needed.

11. How does myelin change action-potential conduction?

Contrast saltatory conduction with conduction in an unmyelinated axon.

Check key points

Myelin insulates axon segments and reduces current leakage. In myelinated axons, the action potential is regenerated at nodes of Ranvier and appears to jump node to node (saltatory conduction), generally making conduction faster and more energy-efficient. In unmyelinated axons, it is regenerated continuously along the membrane.

12. Trace chemical synaptic signaling and distinguish summation and receptor types.

Include the reflex arc, calcium, neurotransmitter release, spatial/temporal summation, and ionotropic/metabotropic receptors.

Check key points
  • Reflex pathway: receptor → sensory/afferent neuron → spinal circuit (possibly an interneuron) → motor/efferent neuron → effector.
  • An arriving action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; Ca²⁺ triggers vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release. Transmitter binds postsynaptic receptors and is cleared by reuptake, breakdown, or diffusion.
  • Spatial summation combines inputs from different synapses; temporal summation combines repeated inputs close together at one synapse.
  • Ionotropic receptors are ligand-gated channels with generally fast effects. Metabotropic receptors activate G proteins/second messengers, often producing slower, longer-lasting modulation.
