Neuroscience Mixed Mock Questions

Try answering each question in 2–4 sentences, or draw a quick labeled diagram when appropriate. Open the answer key after you attempt each one.

Study tip: For every answer, include three parts: how it works, what it measures/tells us, and one comparison to another concept.

1. Compare CAT/CT, PET, and MRI in terms of how they work and what they measure best.

Check key points

CAT/CT uses X-rays across many angles to reconstruct anatomy. PET injects a radioactive tracer to map metabolic or biochemical activity. MRI uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency pulses to produce high-detail tissue images without ionizing radiation. CAT/CT and MRI are strongest for structure, while PET is strongest for biochemical/metabolic activity.

2. Explain the difference between structural imaging and functional imaging.

Check key points

Structural imaging shows anatomy, such as tissue density or shape. Functional imaging measures temporary activity or change, such as blood flow or metabolic activity, which is often correlated with neural activity. MRI and CT are mostly structural; PET and fMRI are functional or activity-related.

3. Why are lesion studies useful, and what are their main limitations?

Check key points

Lesion studies can show that a brain area is important for a given function if damage impairs it. Their limits include the possibility that damage affects multiple pathways, compensation occurs, and behavior may depend on distributed networks rather than a single structure.

4. How do drug studies help researchers understand brain function?

Check key points

Drugs alter neurotransmitter signaling, receptor activity, enzymes, or transporters. By observing how these changes affect behavior or physiology, researchers infer the role of the targeted system in brain function.

5. How do genetic manipulations help researchers study the brain?

Check key points

Researchers can alter, silence, or overexpress genes to test whether a gene contributes to development, behavior, or neural function. This helps identify genetic contributions but can be complicated by compensation and developmental effects.

6. Describe the divisions of the nervous system and identify their general function.

Check key points

The nervous system is divided into the CNS and PNS. The CNS includes the brain and spinal cord, which integrate sensory information and coordinate responses. The PNS carries information to and from the CNS and includes sensory/afferent pathways and motor/efferent pathways.

7. Compare the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems.

Check key points

The sympathetic division mobilizes the body for action, often increasing heart rate and reducing digestive activity. The parasympathetic division supports maintenance and recovery, often slowing heart rate and supporting digestion. Sympathetic is “fight or flight,” parasympathetic is “rest and digest.”

8. Draw or describe a spinal cord cross-section and label the gray matter, white matter, dorsal horn, ventral horn, and roots.

Check key points

Gray matter is the inner butterfly-shaped region. Dorsal horns receive sensory input; ventral horns contain motor neurons. White matter surrounds the gray matter and contains tracts. Dorsal roots carry sensory neurons; ventral roots carry motor neurons.

9. List the major divisions of the brain and describe their general function.

Check key points

The cerebrum handles perception, voluntary action, memory, language, and planning. The diencephalon includes the thalamus and hypothalamus, which relay signals and regulate homeostasis. The brainstem handles arousal and vital autonomic functions. The cerebellum contributes to balance, coordination, timing, and motor learning.

10. Name the four lobes of the cerebral cortex and give one main function for each.

Check key points

Frontal lobe: planning, decision-making, voluntary movement. Parietal lobe: body sensation and spatial processing. Temporal lobe: hearing, language, memory, emotion. Occipital lobe: visual processing.

11. Draw a neuron and label the dendrites, cell body, axon, myelin, and axon terminals.

Check key points

A neuron has dendrites that receive input, a cell body/soma that integrates signals, an axon that carries the signal away, and axon terminals that release neurotransmitter. Myelin surrounds axon segments and helps speed conduction.

12. Explain the resting membrane potential and why it is negative.

Check key points

The resting potential is about -70 mV because the inside of the neuron is more negative than the outside. This is created by selective permeability, especially to K+, and by the Na+/K+ pump, which moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in, helping maintain unequal ion distributions.

13. Describe the steps of an action potential from threshold to repolarization.

Check key points

When depolarization reaches threshold, voltage-gated Na+ channels open and Na+ rapidly enters the cell. This produces the rising phase. Na+ channels then inactivate, voltage-gated K+ channels open, and K+ leaves the cell. This produces repolarization and usually a brief hyperpolarization afterward.

14. What is saltatory conduction, and how is it different from conduction in an unmyelinated axon?

Check key points

Saltatory conduction occurs when the action potential jumps from node to node in a myelinated axon. In unmyelinated axons, conduction is continuous along the membrane and is slower. Myelination increases speed and efficiency.

15. Diagram a synapse and explain how neurotransmitter release occurs.

Check key points

When an action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal, voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open. Ca2+ triggers vesicle fusion with the membrane, so neurotransmitter is released by exocytosis into the synaptic cleft. The neurotransmitter diffuses to receptors on the postsynaptic cell.

16. Differentiate between spatial and temporal summation.

Check key points

Spatial summation is the combination of postsynaptic potentials from different synapses arriving at about the same time. Temporal summation is the combination of repeated potentials from the same synapse arriving closely in time. Both contribute to whether a neuron reaches threshold.

17. Compare ionotropic and metabotropic receptors.

Check key points

Ionotropic receptors are ligand-gated ion channels, so neurotransmitter binding directly opens the channel and effects are typically fast. Metabotropic receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors and produce slower but often longer-lasting intracellular signaling.

18. Why is the blood-brain barrier important, and what structures help form it?

Check key points

The blood-brain barrier protects the brain by limiting entry of many pathogens, toxins, and blood-borne fluctuations while allowing selected nutrients and signaling molecules through. It is formed mainly by endothelial cells with tight junctions, supported by pericytes, the basement membrane, and astrocyte end-feet.

Final advice: for each answer, try to say what it is, how it works, and what it tells us. That is the format most likely to score well in short-answer neuroscience questions.
