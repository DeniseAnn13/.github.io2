Neuroscience Study Guide

1. Introduction: What is neuroscience and how do we study it?

Neuroscience is the scientific study of the nervous system: its structure, function, development, chemistry, disorders, and relationship to behavior and experience. It combines methods from biology, psychology, medicine, chemistry, physics, and computation.

Neuroscientists study questions at several levels: molecules, cells, circuits, behavior, and cognition.

Common brain myths and why they are wrong

  • “We only use 10% of our brain.” False. Brain activity is widespread, and many regions are active even at rest.
  • “People are either left-brained or right-brained.” Not in a simple sense; both hemispheres contribute to most tasks.
  • “People have fixed learning styles.” Preferences may exist, but evidence does not support fixed learning-type labels as a major determinant of learning success.
  • “Adult brains cannot change.” False. Neuroplasticity continues throughout life.
  • “Brain scans can read thoughts.” No. They measure indirect signals, not direct thoughts or intentions.
  • “A single brain area controls a complex behavior.” Most behaviors emerge from networks.

Blood-brain barrier

The BBB is a selective barrier between blood and brain tissue. It is built from endothelial cells joined by tight junctions, plus support from pericytes, the basement membrane, and astrocyte end-feet.

Function: maintains a stable chemical environment for neurons and restricts many pathogens and toxins.

4. Within cells: electrical properties of neurons

Mechanism for creating the resting potential

A typical neuron’s resting membrane potential is about -70 mV. This results from unequal ion distributions and selective permeability, especially to K+.

  1. The Na+/K+ pump moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in.
  2. K+ leak channels allow K+ to leave the cell.
  3. Negative charge accumulates inside, opposing further K+ efflux.
  4. The balance of chemical and electrical gradients sets the resting membrane potential.

Depolarization is a move toward less negative; hyperpolarization is a move more negative than resting.

How an action potential is generated

  1. Inputs depolarize the neuron; if threshold is reached near the axon initial segment, an action potential begins.
  2. Voltage-gated Na+ channels open rapidly; Na+ rushes in.
  3. Na+ channels inactivate and voltage-gated K+ channels open.
  4. K+ leaves, causing repolarization.
  5. K+ channels close slowly, producing a brief after-hyperpolarization.
  6. The action potential propagates along the axon as adjacent membrane is depolarized.

Action potentials are all-or-none once threshold is reached.

Myelin and saltatory conduction

Myelin insulates axons and reduces current leakage. In myelinated axons, the action potential jumps from node to node in a process called saltatory conduction. In unmyelinated axons, conduction occurs continuously along the membrane.

Saltatory conduction is faster and generally more energy-efficient.

5. Between cells: chemical signaling

Reflex arc

Stimulus → sensory receptor → sensory (afferent) neuron → spinal cord → motor (efferent) neuron → effector → response

A reflex is a rapid, coordinated response. The initial processing can occur in the spinal cord before the brain is involved.

Synapse and synaptic signaling

Presynaptic neuron → action potential → Ca2+ entry → vesicle fusion → neurotransmitter release → receptor binding → postsynaptic response → clearance
  1. An action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal.
  2. Voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open and Ca2+ enters.
  3. Neurotransmitter is released by exocytosis.
  4. Neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft.
  5. It binds a receptor on the postsynaptic cell.
  6. Postsynaptic channels or second-messenger pathways change activity.
  7. Signal is terminated by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion.

Spatial and temporal summation

  • Spatial summation: multiple inputs from different synapses arrive together and combine.
  • Temporal summation: repeated inputs from the same synapse arrive close together in time and combine.

Ionotropic receptors

Ionotropic receptors are ligand-gated ion channels. Neurotransmitter binding directly opens the channel. Responses are usually fast.

Metabotropic receptors

Metabotropic receptors do not form ion channels themselves. They activate G proteins and second messenger systems, leading to slower but often longer-lasting effects.

12 Short-Answer Practice Questions

Try answering each in a few clear sentences or by drawing a labeled diagram.

1. Compare CAT/CT, PET, and MRI.

Briefly explain how each works, what it measures best, and one limitation.

Check key points
  • CAT/CT reconstructs anatomy from X-rays; useful for structural density differences, but uses ionizing radiation.
  • PET maps a radioactive tracer to measure a chosen metabolic or biochemical process; has lower spatial/temporal resolution and radiation exposure.
  • MRI uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency signals to construct detailed images without ionizing radiation; structural MRI does not directly measure neural firing.

2. Compare lesion, drug, and genetic studies.

What does each method change, and what can its results tell us?

Check key points
  • Lesions disrupt tissue; impaired function suggests the damaged area or pathway may be necessary.
  • Drugs alter chemical signaling; effects reveal how a signaling system contributes to function.
  • Genetic manipulations alter gene expression; they test gene contribution, but compensation and off-target effects may complicate conclusions.

3. Correct two common myths about the brain.

Name two myths and provide evidence-based corrections.

Check key points

Examples: not only 10% of the brain is used; both hemispheres contribute to most tasks; not all brains are fixed; brain scans do not read thoughts directly; complex behaviors arise from networks.

4. Diagram or describe the divisions of the nervous system.

Include CNS, PNS, sensory/afferent, motor/efferent, somatic, and autonomic branches.

Check key points

CNS = brain and spinal cord. PNS carries information between CNS and body. Sensory/afferent signals travel toward the CNS; motor/efferent commands travel away. The motor division includes somatic (skeletal muscle) and autonomic (organs, glands, smooth and cardiac muscle).

5. Contrast sympathetic and parasympathetic activity.

Give their general roles and one example of how each affects the body.

Check key points

Sympathetic activity mobilizes the body for demands (“fight or flight”), often increasing heart rate and reducing digestion. Parasympathetic supports maintenance and digestion (“rest and digest”), often slowing heart rate and supporting gut activity.

6. Draw a transverse spinal-cord section and label key structures.

Include dorsal and ventral sides, gray and white matter, horns, the central canal, and roots.

Check key points

Gray matter is inner and butterfly-shaped. Dorsal horns receive sensory input; ventral horns contain motor neurons; lateral horns contain autonomic neurons in relevant segments. White matter surrounds it. Dorsal and ventral roots join to form the spinal nerve.

7. Name major brain divisions and their general functions.

Include cerebrum, diencephalon, brainstem, and cerebellum.

Check key points
  • Cerebrum: perception, voluntary action, memory, language, planning.
  • Diencephalon: thalamic relay and hypothalamic homeostasis/endocrine/autonomic regulation.
  • Brainstem: relay, arousal, and vital autonomic control.
  • Cerebellum: coordination, balance, timing, and motor learning.

8. Label the four cerebral lobes and give a general function for each.

A simple side-view drawing is encouraged.

Check key points
  • Frontal: planning, executive function, voluntary movement.
  • Parietal: body sensation and spatial processing.
  • Temporal: hearing, language, memory, emotion.
  • Occipital: vision.

9. Identify neuron parts, action-potential direction, and myelinating glia.

Include dendrites, soma, axon, terminals, and CNS/PNS myelin cells.

Check key points

Typical flow: dendrites → soma → axon initial segment → axon → terminals. Oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS axons; Schwann cells myelinate PNS axons. Astrocytes support neurons and help maintain the BBB.

10. Explain the resting potential and the stages of an action potential.

Mention ion gradients, threshold, channels, and refractory periods.

Check key points
  • Unequal ion distributions and selective permeability—especially K+ leak—produce a negative resting voltage. The Na+/K+ pump maintains gradients.
  • Threshold opens voltage-gated Na+ channels; Na+ influx depolarizes.
  • Na+ channels inactivate and K+ channels open; K+ efflux repolarizes and may cause undershoot.
  • Absolute refractory: spike cannot start; relative refractory: stronger input needed.

11. How does myelin change action-potential conduction?

Contrast saltatory conduction with conduction in an unmyelinated axon.

Check key points

Myelin insulates axon segments and reduces current leakage. In myelinated axons, the action potential is regenerated at nodes of Ranvier and appears to jump from node to node (saltatory conduction). This is typically faster and more energy efficient than conduction in unmyelinated axons.

12. Trace chemical synaptic signaling and distinguish summation and receptor types.

Include the reflex arc, calcium, neurotransmitter release, spatial/temporal summation, and ionotropic/metabotropic receptors.

Check key points
  • Receptor → sensory/afferent neuron → spinal circuit → motor/efferent neuron → effector.
  • An action potential opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels; Ca2+ triggers vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release.
  • Spatial summation combines inputs from different synapses; temporal summation combines repeated inputs close together at one synapse.
  • Ionotropic receptors are ligand-gated channels with fast effects; metabotropic receptors activate G proteins and second messengers with slower, longer-lasting effects.
