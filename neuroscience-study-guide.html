High-Yield Final Review Sheet

Fast exam strategy: define the term, explain how it works, state what it measures or tells us, and compare it to another method or structure.

1. What is neuroscience?

Neuroscience is the study of the nervous system: its structure, function, development, chemistry, disorders, and relationship to behavior and experience. It spans molecular, cellular, circuit, behavioral, and cognitive levels.

2. Brain imaging methods

  • CAT/CT: X-rays from many angles reconstruct cross-sectional images. Measures anatomy and tissue density. Best for fractures, bleeding, large structural changes. Limitation: radiation and lower soft-tissue contrast.
  • PET: radioactive tracer tracks metabolism or receptor activity. Measures glucose use or neurotransmitter-related processes. Limitation: radiation and relatively low spatial/temporal resolution.
  • MRI: magnetic fields and radio waves create detailed images of tissue. Measures anatomy and soft tissue structure. Limitation: indirect for neural activity.
  • fMRI: indirect measure of activity through blood oxygenation changes (BOLD signal).

3. Lesion, drug, and genetic studies

  • Lesion studies: damage tissue and test what function is lost. Can show necessity but not always the whole network.
  • Drug studies: alter receptors, enzymes, or signaling molecules. Can show how chemical systems contribute to behavior.
  • Genetic manipulations: alter gene expression to test whether a gene contributes to function or development.

4. Common brain myths

  • “We only use 10% of the brain.” False.
  • “People are left-brained or right-brained.” False; both hemispheres usually contribute.
  • “Adult brains cannot change.” False; neuroplasticity continues.
  • “Brain scans read thoughts.” False; they measure indirect signals.

5. Nervous system divisions

  • CNS: brain + spinal cord.
  • PNS: sensory and motor pathways.
  • Sensory/afferent: toward the CNS.
  • Motor/efferent: away from the CNS.
  • Somatic: voluntary movement.
  • Autonomic: involuntary functions; includes sympathetic and parasympathetic.

7. Spinal cord

  • Gray matter: inner butterfly; dorsal horn = sensory input, ventral horn = motor neurons, lateral horn = autonomic neurons.
  • White matter: outer tracts.
  • Dorsal root: sensory fibers.
  • Ventral root: motor fibers.

8. Major brain divisions

  • Cerebrum: perception, voluntary action, language, memory, planning.
  • Diencephalon: thalamus and hypothalamus; relay and homeostasis.
  • Brainstem: arousal, relay, and vital autonomic functions.
  • Cerebellum: coordination, balance, timing, motor learning.

9. Lobes

  • Frontal: planning, executive control, movement.
  • Parietal: body sensation, spatial processing.
  • Temporal: hearing, language, memory, emotion.
  • Occipital: vision.

10. Neurons and glia

  • Neuron: dendrites receive input, soma integrates, axon carries signal, terminals release transmitter.
  • Oligodendrocytes: myelin in CNS.
  • Schwann cells: myelin in PNS.
  • Astrocytes: support neurons; help maintain BBB.
  • Microglia: immune surveillance.

11. Resting potential and action potentials

  • Resting potential is about -70 mV.
  • Na+/K+ pump maintains gradients: 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in.
  • Threshold is reached; voltage-gated Na+ channels open; depolarization occurs.
  • Na+ channels inactivate; K+ channels open; repolarization occurs.
  • After-hyperpolarization may occur. Absolute refractory = cannot fire; relative refractory = stronger input needed.

12. Myelin and conduction

Myelin insulates axons. In myelinated axons, action potentials jump between nodes of Ranvier; this is saltatory conduction. It is faster and more energy efficient than conduction in unmyelinated axons.

13. Reflex arc and synaptic transmission

  • Reflex arc: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord → motor neuron → effector.
  • Synapse: action potential reaches terminal → Ca2+ channels open → vesicles release transmitter → transmitter binds receptor → postsynaptic response → signal ends by reuptake/diffusion/breakdown.

14. Summation and receptors

  • Spatial summation: inputs from different synapses combine.
  • Temporal summation: repeated inputs from one synapse combine in time.
  • Ionotropic receptors: fast, ligand-gated ion channels.
  • Metabotropic receptors: slower, G-protein and second-messenger signaling.
